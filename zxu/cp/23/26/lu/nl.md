# ### Why is the Journal Sub-divided?

The **Journal is sub-divided** into different special journals because a large number of transactions take place in a business every day. Recording all transactions in one journal would be **time-consuming and inconvenient**.

Sub-division of the Journal:

* Saves **time and labour**.
* Makes recording **systematic and convenient**.
* Facilitates **division of work** among accounting staff.
* Makes **posting to the Ledger easier**.
* Helps in **checking and locating errors**.

### Special Journals and Transactions Recorded

| Special Journal                                      | Transactions recorded                                                                                                                                                                                                    |
| ---------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **1. Cash Book**                                     | All **cash and bank transactions**, including cash receipts, cash payments, deposits and withdrawals.                                                                                                                    |
| **2. Purchases Book**                                | **Credit purchases of goods** in which the business deals.                                                                                                                                                               |
| **3. Sales Book**                                    | **Credit sales of goods** in which the business deals.                                                                                                                                                                   |
| **4. Purchases Returns Book** (Returns Outward Book) | Goods **returned to suppliers** out of credit purchases.                                                                                                                                                                 |
| **5. Sales Returns Book** (Returns Inward Book)      | Goods **returned by customers** out of credit sales.                                                                                                                                                                     |
| **6. Bills Receivable Book**                         | Bills of exchange and promissory notes **received from customers/debtors**.                                                                                                                                              |
| **7. Bills Payable Book**                            | Bills of exchange and promissory notes **accepted by the business in favour of creditors**.                                                                                                                              |
| **8. Journal Proper**                                | Transactions that cannot be recorded in the above special journals, such as **opening entries, closing entries, adjustment entries, transfer entries, rectification entries, and credit purchase/sale of fixed assets**. |

**In short:** The Journal is sub-divided mainly to make accounting **quicker, easier, systematic, and suitable for division of work**.

At the end of the accounting year, the branch balances are incorporated into the Head Office books so that the final accounts show the combined results of the Head Office and all branches.

For branch loss:

Profit & Loss Account Dr.
    To Branch Account

### Meaning of Accounting Concepts

**Accounting concepts** are the basic assumptions, principles, and rules that provide a foundation for recording and presenting business transactions in the books of accounts. They guide an accountant in deciding **what, when, and how transactions should be recorded**.

The important accounting concepts that guide the accountant at the **recording stage** are:

1. **Business Entity Concept**
   The business is treated as a separate entity from its owner. Therefore, only transactions relating to the business are recorded in the business books.

2. **Money Measurement Concept**
   Only those transactions which can be **measured in monetary terms** are recorded in the books of accounts.

3. **Going Concern Concept**
   It is assumed that the business will continue its operations for the foreseeable future. Therefore, assets are generally recorded on the basis that they will be used in the business rather than immediately sold.

4. **Accounting Period Concept**
   The life of a business is divided into specific periods, usually one year, so that its **profit or loss can be determined periodically**.

5. **Cost Concept**
   Assets are generally recorded in the books at their **acquisition cost**, including costs necessary to bring them into use.

6. **Dual Aspect Concept**
   Every business transaction has **two aspects**. Therefore, every transaction affects at least two accounts and maintains the accounting equation:
   **Assets = Liabilities + Capital.**

7. **Accrual Concept**
   Transactions are recorded when **income is earned or expenses are incurred**, rather than only when cash is received or paid.

8. **Realisation Concept**
   Revenue is recognised when it is **earned or realised**, generally when goods or services are provided and the right to receive consideration arises.

**In short:** Accounting concepts provide a common and logical basis for recording transactions and help maintain **consistency, accuracy, and reliability** in accounting.

### Need and Significance of Depreciation

**Depreciation** means the gradual and permanent decrease in the value of a fixed asset due to **wear and tear, passage of time, obsolescence, or other causes**.

#### Need and Significance

1. **Determination of True Profit or Loss**
   Depreciation is an expense. Charging it to the Profit and Loss Account helps determine the **true profit or loss** of the business.

2. **Correct Valuation of Assets**
   Depreciation ensures that fixed assets are shown in the Balance Sheet at their **appropriate book value**.

3. **Replacement of Assets**
   Depreciation helps in retaining a portion of the profits within the business, which can assist in **replacing assets** when they become unusable.

4. **Matching Cost with Revenue**
   The cost of an asset is spread over its useful life. Thus, depreciation matches the **cost of using the asset with the revenue earned from it**.

5. **Determination of Cost of Production**
   Depreciation on machinery and other production assets forms part of the **cost of production**.

6. **Compliance with Accounting Principles**
   Charging depreciation is necessary for presenting a **true and fair view** of the financial position and performance of the business.

### Factors to be Considered in Determining Depreciation

The following factors are important:

1. **Original Cost of the Asset** – The purchase price plus expenses incurred to acquire and install the asset.
2. **Estimated Useful Life** – The expected period for which the asset will be used.
3. **Residual/Scrap Value** – The estimated value of the asset at the end of its useful life.
4. **Expected Usage** – The extent to which the asset is expected to be used.
5. **Obsolescence** – Technological changes may reduce the useful life of an asset.
6. **Repairs and Maintenance** – The condition and expected maintenance requirements of the asset may affect its useful life.
7. **Method of Depreciation** – The appropriate method, such as the **Straight-Line Method** or **Written-Down Value Method**, should be selected.

**Formula under the Straight-Line Method:**

[
\text{Annual Depreciation} =
\frac{\text{Cost of Asset} - \text{Estimated Residual Value}}
{\text{Estimated Useful Life}}
]

Thus, depreciation is essential for **correct profit measurement and proper valuation of fixed assets**.
